# Best LeetCode Course: Pattern Templates, Real Prices, and How to Choose One in 4–8 Weeks

Most people searching for the best LeetCode course have the same problem, and it isn't a lack of resources. LeetCode itself has 3,000+ problems, a discussion forum, and free explore cards. NeetCode has a free YouTube channel with 150 curated problems explained on video. There are Udemy courses for $12 on sale. So why does it still feel like nothing is working?

Because the thing most candidates need isn't a bigger pile of problems. It's a decision framework: given a problem you've never seen, which approach is the right one? That's the skill interviewers actually test, and it's the thing a problem bank will never teach you directly.

This guide covers what the main paid options actually give you, what AlgoMonster currently costs, and where its limits are. If you're deciding whether to pay for a course at all, that's the useful question.

## What "best" means depends on which gap you have

There are three separate formats on the market, and they are not interchangeable.

**Problem banks** (LeetCode, and the free side of just about everything) give you volume. They assume you'll infer the patterns by repetition. Cheap, effective if you already know what to study.

**Video courses** (NeetCode Pro, AlgoExpert) walk through solutions on screen. Great if you learn by watching, slower to search and reference later.

**Pattern courses** (AlgoMonster, Grokking-style content on Educative and Design Gurus) start with the pattern, explain when it applies, hand you a reusable code template, then drill variations. This is the format that targets the recognition gap.

If you've solved 150 problems and still freeze for 15 minutes at the start of a new one, you don't need more volume. If you've solved 20 problems and can't implement a hash map, no pattern course will save you, because those courses assume you can already write code in at least one language.

## The limits, stated plainly

AlgoMonster is not a complete interview solution, and its own marketing doesn't pretend otherwise. Reviews converge on the same three gaps.

**No live interview simulation.** You work at your own pace, with no timer, no one watching, and no follow-up questions. Real screens are 45 minutes with a senior engineer who will ask how your complexity changes if the input doesn't fit in memory. Learning the patterns and performing them under observation are different skills.

**System design stops at introductory.** There's some coverage, not enough for senior loops where a dedicated design round is standard. If you're targeting L5 and above, budget separate hours for distributed systems, database design, and scaling tradeoffs.

**Behavioral prep is thin.** Amazon's Leadership Principles and Meta's impact-driven questions need their own preparation. A paragraph of tips doesn't replace STAR practice.

Add the smaller problem count relative to LeetCode (roughly 230 problems versus 3,000+) and the absence of video, and you get a clear picture: AlgoMonster is a DSA phase tool, not a prep stack.

## Who should pay, and who shouldn't

You'll get your money's worth if all three of these are true:

1. You can already write working code in one language.
2. Your failure mode is picking the wrong approach, not implementing the right one.
3. You have roughly 4–8 weeks and can put in 1–2 hours a day.

Skip it if you're two weeks from an interview (not enough time for 48 templates to stick), if you're still shaky on fundamentals like recursion or hash maps, if you need deep system design coverage, or if you know you learn better from video. In those cases NeetCode's free tier or a $15 Udemy course is a better use of the money.

For the rest, the arithmetic is simple. $99 for a year is less than two months of LeetCode Premium at $35/month, and the lifetime plan at $189 is a one-time payment that survives your next two job searches. Whether that's worth it depends entirely on how often you plan to interview again. Engineers who job-hop every two to three years will use the lifetime plan more than once; someone doing a single one-time transition should take the annual option and keep the $90.

## How to sequence it: patterns first, volume second

The most common successful pattern reported by candidates is combining the two tools rather than picking a side.

Weeks 1–3: run the core patterns on AlgoMonster. Two pointers, sliding window, binary search variants, BFS/DFS, hash map patterns. The goal is recognizing the pattern within a couple of minutes of reading a problem.

Weeks 3–6: move your practice to LeetCode, using company-tagged sets if you're targeting specific employers. At this point you're not learning, you're building speed on a framework you already have.

Final week before the interview: Speedrun mode to refresh the templates, plus timed mock interviews somewhere else, because nothing on AlgoMonster replicates the pressure of someone watching you think.

That combination is why I'd frame AlgoMonster as an efficient first four weeks rather than a substitute for LeetCode. Used that way, it's the cheapest structured entry point currently on the market. Used as a replacement for practice volume, it will leave you under-rehearsed.
